Hiring a contractor in Hawaii is easier when you know what state law requires of them. This guide walks through the steps using the Hawaii contractor licensing law (Hawaii Revised Statutes chapter 444), the Contractors License Board rules (Hawaii Administrative Rules chapter 16-77) and consumer guidance from the Department of Commerce and Consumer Affairs (DCCA). It describes what the rules say. It is not legal advice. All sources were checked on November 22, 2026.

## When do you need a licensed contractor in Hawaii?

DCCA says a contractor's license is required for any project exceeding $1,500 in labor and material, and for any project that requires a building, electrical or plumbing permit. [1]

The statute exempts a project whose total contract price for labor and materials is not more than $1,500. That exemption does not apply where a building permit is required, where the work is part of a larger project, or where a project is split into small contracts to avoid the law. [2] DCCA adds that the handyman exemption does not cover electrical or plumbing work. [3]

## What do Class A, B and C licenses mean?

Hawaii classifies contractors as general engineering (A), general building (B) and specialty (C). [4] [5]

- **A, general engineering:** fixed works that need specialized engineering knowledge, such as highways, bridges, sewers and dams. [4]
- **B, general building:** structures that shelter people, animals or property and that need more than two unrelated building trades or crafts. [4]
- **C, specialty:** work requiring a special skill, such as electrical, plumbing, drywall, painting, flooring or roofing. [4]

For a house, an addition or a remodel with several trades, DCCA says that if a project involves more than two specialties you should consider a general contractor, who is licensed to do most of the work and hires licensed subcontractors for the specialty work. [6] Honolulu's permitting department says a general contractor is typically required when more than two specialty contractors are needed. [7]

## Step 1: Check the license and complaint history

- Search the license. DCCA's Professional and Vocational License Search shows license status, classifications and proof of insurance. [6] The search is at [mypvl.dcca.hawaii.gov/public-license-search](https://mypvl.dcca.hawaii.gov/public-license-search). [8] Search by the license ID the state uses, which for contractors starts with CT. A class code such as BC is not the license number and returns no result.
- Check complaints. The Regulated Industries Complaints Office (RICO) complaint history search shows complaints against licensees and unlicensed contracting investigations. [6]
- Call if you prefer. The DCCA Consumer Resource Center number is 1-844-808-3222. [6]
- Match the person to the license. DCCA warns that some unlicensed operators use fake license numbers or numbers that belong to someone else, and suggests asking for photo ID. [6]

DCCA notes that these checks are not a guarantee, but they are a useful step. [6]

A license tells you something. To qualify, applicants must show training and experience, a good reputation for honesty and fair dealing, tax clearances and proof of insurance. [6] DCCA says a licensed contractor has workers' compensation and liability insurance, which protects the homeowner if someone is injured or property is damaged. [1]

## Step 2: Get bids you can compare

DCCA's top tips for construction projects include these. [3]

- Set a budget in advance and keep a reserve for changes or unexpected costs.
- Get at least three bids for the same work and materials, and ask why if the amounts vary a lot.
- Ask for references, look at completed projects and speak with past clients.

## Step 3: Know the disclosures you are owed

Before or when a homeowner signs a contract for home construction or improvements, a licensed contractor must do three things. [9]

1. Explain verbally and in detail the lien rights of everyone performing under the contract, including the homeowner, the contractor, subcontractors and material suppliers.
2. Explain your option to demand bonding on the project, how a bond would protect you and about what it would cost.
3. Disclose all information about the contract and its performance.

The board's rules add that the contractor must disclose the scope of work, the approximate percentage to be subcontracted, whether the contractor is bonded, the license number and classifications, any warranties and an estimate of the cost. [10]

## Step 4: Read the written contract

State rules say a contractor must give a homeowner a written contract that includes the following. [11] In May 2026 the Hawaii Intermediate Court of Appeals held that item 2, the exact dollar amount, is invalid because state lien law also allows cost plus contracts. The same court held that item 5 is valid, and that a home construction contract missing the subcontracting percentage and the subcontractors' names and license numbers is unenforceable. [16]

1. The contractor's name, address, license number and classifications
2. The exact dollar amount you owe under the contract (struck down by the 2026 ruling)
3. The start date and the number of days to completion
4. The work to be done and the materials to be used
5. The approximate percentage of work to be subcontracted, with each subcontractor's name and license number
6. A notice about the risk of loss for payments made to a sales representative
7. An explanation of the lien rights of everyone performing under the contract
8. The terms of any warranty offered
9. The signatures of the homeowner and the contractor

The contract must be signed before any work begins. [11] The statute also requires notice of the contractor's right to resolve alleged construction defects before litigation. [9] A rule exception covers service work that needs no county permit, such as routine maintenance. [11]

DCCA also recommends, but the rules do not require, terms such as a payment schedule tied to completed work, who obtains and pays for permits, lien releases from subcontractors and suppliers, penalties for delay, how either party may cancel, a dispute resolution method, and the plans and specifications attached to the contract. [12]

## Step 5: Pay as the work is done

DCCA's advice is to never pay all of the money up front, to tie payments to completed work and to hold back 5 to 10 percent until all work is done. [12] It also suggests getting a receipt for every payment, putting change orders in writing, and collecting lien releases from subcontractors and suppliers as they are paid. [3] DCCA's brochure does not name a specific down payment percentage. [12]

## Step 6: Settle permits before work starts

Ask who will handle the building, electrical and plumbing permits. [3] Licensed contractors can apply for and obtain building permits, and DCCA warns that an unlicensed contractor who asks you to pull an owner-builder permit may be unable to get a permit for you. [6]

Electrical and plumbing work has its own rule. Electrical and plumbing contractors must employ licensed electricians and plumbers to do the actual work. [1] Honolulu's permitting department says all electrical and plumbing work must be done by state-licensed contractors and that owners cannot do it themselves. [7] Permit rules are set by each county, so check with your county permitting office before you start.

## What protection do you have if something goes wrong?

- **Contractors Recovery Fund.** The fund may pay up to $12,500 per contract, with a maximum of $25,000 for any one contractor. It applies only if the contractor was actively licensed when the contract was signed. You need a court judgment against the contractor and must notify the Contractors License Board in writing when a lawsuit starts. DCCA states that recovery is not automatic. [13]
- **Unlicensed contractors.** The statute bars a person who did not have a license before contracting from recovering payment in a civil action for that work. [14]
- **Disclosure violations.** A violation of the disclosure and contract law is treated as an unfair or deceptive practice. [9]
- **Complaints.** RICO takes complaints and investigates unlicensed contracting. [6]

## What are the warning signs?

DCCA lists door-to-door sales pitches, pressure to decide today, and requests for cash, a large down payment or full payment in advance. [6]

## FAQ

### How do I check a contractor's license in Hawaii?

Use DCCA's license search at [mypvl.dcca.hawaii.gov/public-license-search](https://mypvl.dcca.hawaii.gov/public-license-search) or call 1-844-808-3222. [6] [8]

### Do I need a licensed contractor for a small job?

A license is required when the job is over $1,500 in labor and materials or needs a building, electrical or plumbing permit. [1] [2]

### What is a Class B contractor license?

It is the general building classification, for structures that need more than two unrelated building trades or crafts. [4]

### How much should I pay up front?

DCCA gives no set percentage. It says never pay everything up front, pay as work is completed and hold back 5 to 10 percent until the end. [12]

### Can I act as my own general contractor?

State law has an owner-builder exemption for structures built for your own use and not offered for sale or lease, and it does not cover electrical or plumbing work that must be done by licensed persons. [15] An owner-builder cannot recover from the Contractors Recovery Fund. [15]

### What is the Contractors Recovery Fund?

A fund paid for by licensed contractors that may pay a homeowner up to $12,500 per contract after a court judgment against a licensed contractor. [13]

### Who is responsible for getting the permits?

DCCA says contractors do not have to obtain or pay for permits unless the contract says so, which is why the contract should name who does. [12]
